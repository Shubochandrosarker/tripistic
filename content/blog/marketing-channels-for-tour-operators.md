Every operator gets the same advice: post more on Instagram, run some ads, get reviews. Some of that is right. Here is what the numbers usually look like once you separate what feels productive from what produces bookings.

## The ranking

| Channel | Cost | Time to payback | Compounds? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Repeat customers | Near zero | Immediate | Strongly |
| Reviews | Time only | 1–3 months | Strongly |
| Local SEO | Time or agency fee | 3–6 months | Strongly |
| Referral partners (hotels, concierges) | Commission | 1–2 months | Moderately |
| Content and organic search | Time | 6–12 months | Strongly |
| Email to past travellers | Near zero | Immediate | Moderately |
| Marketplaces | 20–30% commission | Immediate | No |
| Paid social | Ad spend | Immediate, stops instantly | No |
| Paid search | Ad spend | Immediate, stops instantly | No |

The pattern: **the cheapest channels are the ones you already earned and are not using.**

## The channels operators under-use

### Repeat customers

A traveller who had a great trip is the cheapest booking you will ever get, and most operators never contact them again. If you know who travelled with you and when, a well-timed message a year later converts several times better than any cold traffic.

The blocker is almost never strategy. It is that the booking data and the customer record live in different places, so nobody can produce the list.

### Reviews, systematically

Reviews are the highest-leverage marketing asset in travel and most operators collect them by accident. Systematic collection means: ask every traveller, 24–48 hours after the trip, with a direct link, automatically — and suppress the request when something went wrong.

Going from occasional to systematic typically multiplies review volume several times over within a season. That moves marketplace ranking, local search ranking, and direct-site conversion simultaneously.

### Local SEO

For "things to do in [city]" and "[activity] [city]" searches, the businesses that rank have three things: a complete and current business profile, consistent name/address/phone across directories, and review velocity. None of that requires an agency. All of it requires someone to actually do it.

### Referral partners

Hotel concierges, boutique accommodation, DMCs, and local restaurants send high-intent travellers who are already in your city with time to fill. A 10–15% referral commission beats 25% marketplace commission and builds a relationship that compounds.

Track referrals properly — by company, with revenue attributed — or you will not know which partners to nurture.

## The channels operators over-use

### Paid social for cold acquisition

Paid social works for **retargeting** people who visited your booking page. It works poorly for cold acquisition in travel, because the purchase requires trust and specific dates. Operators routinely spend months of budget discovering this.

Where it does work: retargeting abandoned checkouts, and lookalike audiences built from actual past customers rather than page engagers.

### Posting without a destination

An Instagram account with good photos and no booking path converts nothing. Before increasing posting frequency, check that the link goes to a live availability calendar, that the mobile checkout works in under 90 seconds, and that a traveller can complete a booking without emailing you.

Fixing the destination usually beats increasing the traffic.

## Sequencing

**If you are starting from zero, in order:**

1. Fix the direct booking flow. Everything else feeds it.
2. Turn on systematic review collection.
3. Complete and maintain your local business profiles.
4. Build the post-trip email sequence.
5. Sign two or three referral partners.
6. Only then consider paid.

Steps 1–5 cost time, not money, and compound. Step 6 costs money and stops the day you stop paying.

## Measuring honestly

Track **cost per booking by source**, and **revenue by source over 24 months** rather than at first booking. A marketplace booking at 25% commission that never returns looks fine on day one and poor over two years. A referral partner booking at 12% that produces two repeat direct bookings looks the opposite.

You cannot run that analysis unless lead source is captured on the booking and the customer record persists across trips. Which is, again, a data problem before it is a marketing problem.
